\begin{abstract}

	We measure how much performance each of three cache-hierarchy parameters
	(capacity, line size, and associativity) buys for five PolyBench kernels
	simulated in gem5, sweeping one factor at a time from a common baseline (155
	runs) so that every change is attributable to a single parameter. Line size
	dominates: it cuts first-level data cache (L1D) misses almost identically
	across kernels (\(8\times\)), yet yields speedups from 0.5\% to 15.4\%.
	Associativity beyond two ways is inert for these regular accesses, and capacity
	acts as a threshold at the last-level cache. One measured quantity, the fraction
	of execution cycles spent waiting on L1D misses, orders the kernels for both
	effective factors and, via Amdahl's law, predicts the line-size speedups from a
	single baseline run to within 1.4 percentage points. Code and data are public,
	and run-to-run determinism is verified.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The disparity between processor and main memory speeds---the \textit{memory wall}
identified three decades ago \cite{wulf:1995} and still widening
\cite{gholami:2024}---remains the dominant first-order constraint on
single-thread performance, and every modern processor mitigates it with a
multi-level cache hierarchy: private first-level instruction and data caches
(L1I, L1D) backed by larger, slower shared levels up to the last-level cache
(\textsc{llc}). Its parameters---capacity, line (block) size, associativity,
replacement policy, latency---are neither independent nor uniformly important.
The designer's practical question is not whether a larger or more associative
cache helps, but which parameter, at a fixed transistor and energy budget, buys
the most performance for a given class of access patterns, which analysis alone
cannot settle, since the effects interact through miss classification and
compute intensity.

The literature offers abundant evidence on individual points of this design space
but little that lets a practitioner rank the parameters. Classic trace-driven
studies established how each parameter moves the \textit{miss ratio}---line size
\cite{smith:1987}, associativity \cite{hill:1989}, both across a benchmark suite
\cite{cantin:2001}---but a miss ratio is not performance: the same miss reduction
is worth different amounts to different programs, and converting it requires
charging each miss its actual latency. Recent simulation work
either fixes the hierarchy and varies one dimension \cite{khoshavi:2018}, sweeps
many parameters for one core and reports aggregate performance
\cite{shahid:2025}, or shows that cache conclusions shift by more than 30\% with
the measurement procedure alone \cite{bueno:2024}---yet such procedures are
seldom shipped in executable form. The gap is thus twofold: no
\textit{cross-workload, multi-parameter} performance sensitivity data under one
consistent, timing-accurate methodology, and no \textit{mechanized} pipeline in
which that methodology is itself the artifact.

We close it with a factor-isolating study whose entire pipeline is one
version-pinned build graph executable by a single
command.\footnote{All code, configurations, raw gem5 output, and this manuscript
are public at \url{https://github.com/lucca-pellegrini/AC3-TP1} (tag
\texttt{v0.1.2}); one command re-derives every number and figure here.} Varying
one factor at a time from a common baseline, we sweep 11 multi-level capacity
presets derived from shipping commercial \textsc{cpu}s, line sizes from 32 to 256
bytes, and associativities from direct-mapped to 16-way per level, over five
PolyBench~4.2.1 kernels \cite{polybench:2016}: dense streaming (\textsc{ata}x,
\textsc{gemm}), dynamic programming (Floyd-Warshall), and iterative stencils
(Jacobi-2D, Seidel-2D). This totals 155 gem5 \cite{gem5-20:2007} simulations,
each instrumented through \textit{libm5} so that statistics cover exactly the
region of interest (\textsc{roi}).

The three factors differ by more than an order of magnitude in leverage: line
size buys up to \(+15.4\%\) \textsc{ipc}, capacity up to \(+7.2\%\), and
associativity beyond two ways at most \(0.2\%\); but the ranking matters less
than its explanation. Our contributions are (i)~an experimental design in which
every measured change is attributable to a single cache parameter; (ii)~a
predictive criterion: one measured quantity, \(f_{\text{stall}}\), orders the
five kernels for both effective factors and predicts the line-size speedups from
one baseline run, with the residual explained; and (iii)~a reproducible artifact
with run-to-run determinism verified, including null results usually discarded.
For the five kernels studied, this inverts the intuitive ordering of cache
parameters: the gains exploit spatial locality, not conflict avoidance or
capacity.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

The parameters we sweep were first characterized by trace-driven simulation.
\cite{smith:1987} found the miss-ratio reduction from longer lines
\enquote{relatively stable across workloads}, with an optimum of 16--64 bytes set
by the memory of the time; our timing-accurate sweep reproduces that stability
(a near-exact \(8\times\) miss reduction in every kernel), shows that its
performance value nonetheless varies 30-fold, and finds his transfer-time
penalty reappearing at 256-byte lines. \cite{hill:1989} quantified
associativity: direct-mapped to two-way removes most conflict misses, and each
further doubling yields diminishing but nonzero returns on general-purpose
traces; our regular kernels are the limiting case in which returns beyond two
ways vanish. \cite{cantin:2001} tabulated miss ratios over capacity and
associativity for \textsc{spec cpu2000}, the closest precedent to a
cross-workload, multi-parameter survey, but reports miss ratios only, holds line
size fixed, and offers no quantity explaining why benchmarks differ. We report
performance, sweep three parameters, and supply that quantity.

That quantity, \(f_{\text{stall}}\), is not new in form: it is the memory-stall
term of the classic decomposition \(\text{\textsc{cpu} time} =
(\text{\textsc{cpu} cycles} + \text{memory-stall cycles}) \times \text{cycle
time}\) \cite{hennessy:2019}, expressed as a fraction and \textit{measured}
rather than modeled, since in a blocking core gem5's summed miss latency is
exactly the stall time. It differs in regime from Roofline \cite{williams:2009},
which bounds throughput by arithmetic intensity against peak bandwidth for
overlapped accesses; our core is latency-bound with one outstanding miss, where
the stall term is additive and Amdahl's law needs no overlap correction. Our
contribution is the empirical demonstration that it orders kernels for two
parameters and predicts the line-size speedup from one run. Closer in time, \cite{khoshavi:2018} replaces \textsc{sram} with
\textsc{stt-ram} and reports a 51.7\% L2 read-miss reduction against one fixed
hierarchy, incomparable with conventional tuning; \cite{shahid:2025} explores the
\textsc{cva6} \textsc{risc-v} design space in gem5 but reports aggregate
performance, not per-level misses; and \cite{bueno:2024} shows replacement-policy
conclusions moving by over 30\% with interval weighting alone, our motivation for
shipping the procedure as an executable artifact.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
\subsection{Experimental Design}\label{sub:rationale}

Every element of the setup follows from the requirement that any observed change
be attributable to exactly one cache parameter, so the three factors are swept
separately from a fixed baseline rather than jointly: bundled commercial
configurations confound them, differing in capacity \textit{and} line size
\textit{and} associativity at once. Capacity is varied as 11 multi-level presets
derived from shipping commercial \textsc{cpu}s (Table~\ref{tbl:cachesizes}),
since per-level capacities are co-designed under area and latency constraints;
being \textit{categorical}, they are treated as one factor and reported as deltas
against the Intel®~Core™~i7-6700K baseline, never attributing an effect to a
specific level. Line size is swept over four power-of-two values from 32 to 256
bytes around a 64-byte baseline, and associativity over 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 ways
independently for L1, L2, and L3 (baselines 8-way for L1/L2, 16-way for L3),
since varying one level at a time localizes conflict pressure.\footnote{Gem5
expects powers of two, so the 12-way configuration of the Zen~5 \textsc{cpu}s
cannot be reproduced.} The direct-mapped case is included as the only
configuration in which every set conflict necessarily evicts.

Three further choices isolate the cache from the core. We use gem5's in-order
\textit{TimingSimpleCPU}, the simplest model that still charges realistic memory
timing: an out-of-order core would overlap miss latency with independent work,
entangling the cache effect with the core's ability to hide it, whereas here
every miss stalls the core for its full latency, so \textsc{cpi} responds
additively, which is what makes the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results} exact. We disable prefetching, which partially
substitutes for line size and would mask the very effect under investigation.
And datasets are reduced per kernel (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}), since 155
runs at \texttt{LARGE\_\-DATA\-SET} would be prohibitive; this compresses the
capacity effects we measure (Section~\ref{sub:capacity}).

\subsection{Simulated System and Metrics}\label{sub:system}

The simulated system pairs the in-order \textit{TimingSimpleCPU} with the x86-64
\textsc{isa} at 4~GHz (250-tick clock period). Private L1I and L1D caches (4-cycle
hit latency) are backed by a shared L2 (12 cycles) and L3 (36 cycles), all with
\textsc{lru} replacement and no prefetcher, joined by crossbars of 32-byte data
width. Main memory is gem5's \texttt{DDR3\_1600\_8x8} model behind one
\texttt{MemCtrl}: a single channel with a 64-bit data bus, two ranks of eight
banks, 8~GiB, \(t_{\textsc{cl}} = t_{\textsc{rcd}} = t_{\textsc{rp}} = 13.75\)~ns,
\textsc{fr-fcfs} scheduling, open-adaptive page policy. Although each cache has
Miss Status Holding Registers (8/20/40 at L1/L2/L3), they admit no memory-level
parallelism here: \textit{TimingSimpleCPU} issues one access at a time and waits
for it, and the controller's mean read-queue occupancy is exactly 1.00 in every
run. Each workload was compiled with
GCC at \texttt{-O3} as a static x86-64 \textsc{elf} against musl \cite{musl} and
gem5's \textit{libm5}, whose calls immediately before and after the kernel
invocation confine statistics to the \textsc{roi}, excluding initialization and
output.

Two consequences of the core model must be stated. Absolute \textsc{ipc} is low
(0.13--0.15, \textsc{cpi} \(\approx 7\)) because \textit{TimingSimpleCPU} is
unpipelined: it serializes the fetch, execution, and data access of each
instruction and pays the full 4-cycle L1 hit latency on every fetch and access,
so 6.5--6.8 cycles per instruction are fixed overhead independent of the cache,
and only relative changes are meaningful. That overhead also inflates the
denominator of every stall fraction: a pipelined in-order core with base
\textsc{cpi} near 1 would show larger sensitivities, and an out-of-order core
smaller ones. The kernel ordering and the additive structure, not the absolute
magnitudes, are the transferable results.

From each run's \textsc{roi} statistics we record \textsc{ipc}, per-level hits,
misses, miss rates, \textsc{mpki} (misses per kilo-instruction), and summed miss
latency; presets are reported as \textsc{ipc} deltas against the per-kernel
baseline. Our explanatory variable is
\(f_{\text{stall}} = \texttt{dcache.overallMissLatency} / (\texttt{numCycles}
\times 250)\), the fraction of \textsc{roi} execution during which the core is
waiting for an L1D miss to be served, whether by L2, L3, or \textsc{dram}.
Because the core blocks on each access, this is exactly the memory-stall term of
the \textsc{cpu}-time decomposition \cite{hennessy:2019}, not an estimate; it
excludes the constant L1 hit latency and the negligible L1I misses. We verified
its additivity: non-stall cycles vary by less than 0.7\% across the entire
line-size sweep of any kernel.

Each configuration was simulated once, which suffices because the simulation is
deterministic; we verified this rather than assumed it. The grid contains six
physically identical baseline configurations per kernel (explicit baseline,
baseline preset, 64-byte line, and 8/8/16-way L1/L2/L3), and all six produce
bit-identical statistics; a second run of the full sweep on another host
reproduced every instruction count exactly, L1D miss counts to 0.01\%, and
cycle counts to \(3 \times 10^{-5}\), far below the smallest effect reported
(\(0.18\%\)).

\subsection{Workload Selection}\label{sub:workloads}

The five PolyBench~4.2.1 \cite{polybench:2016} kernels span the two mechanisms we
identify as decisive, access-pattern spatiality and working-set size;
Table~\ref{tab:workloads} gives datasets and footprints, and the loop structures
below are those of version 4.2.1.
\textsc{ata}x computes \(y = A^{T}(Ax)\) \cite{lawson:1979}: for each row \(i\)
it streams \(A_{i,:}\) twice, for \(\mathit{tmp}_i = A_{i,:}\cdot x\) and for
\(y \mathrel{+}= \mathit{tmp}_i A_{i,:}\), so the 30.4~MiB matrix is read with no
reuse beyond that pair of passes. \textsc{gemm} computes \(C \leftarrow \beta C +
\alpha AB\) in \(i\)-\(k\)-\(j\) order: the inner loop streams rows \(C_{i,:}\)
and \(B_{k,:}\) contiguously, the 1.7~KiB row \(C_{i,:}\) stays in L1D across all
\(k\), and \(B\) (422~KiB) is re-streamed for every \(i\). Floyd-Warshall
computes all-pairs shortest paths \cite{floyd:1962, warshall:1962} over one
\texttt{int} matrix, streaming rows with heavy reuse of row \(k\). Jacobi-2D and
Seidel-2D apply five-point stencils \cite{golub:2013}, touching neighbors at
offsets \(\pm 1\) (same or adjacent line) and \(\pm N\) (adjacent rows, 2--3~KiB
apart, so the three live rows stay in L1D); Jacobi sweeps two arrays alternately,
while Seidel-2D's in-place update adds loop-carried dependencies. Datasets were
reduced until each run completed within days; Floyd-Warshall, with cubic
complexity, required \texttt{SMALL\_\-DATA\-SET}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Overview and Ranking of the Three Factors}\label{sub:overview}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} summarizes the sweep as how much \textsc{ipc} each
factor buys per kernel: line size moves it by up to \(15.4\%\), capacity by up to
\(7.2\%\), and associativity beyond two ways by at most \(0.2\%\). The kernel
ordering is the same for line size and capacity, suggesting one quantity governs
both, and the mechanism is uniform where the outcome is not: L1D \textsc{mpki}
falls almost exactly \(8.0\times\) for four of five kernels while \textsc{ipc}
gains span \(0.5\%\) to \(15.4\%\).

The gains differ because each kernel exposes a different fraction of execution
to miss latency. That fraction, \(f_{\text{stall}}\), ranges from \(0.004\)
(Floyd-Warshall) to \(0.090\) (\textsc{ata}x), a 25-fold spread, and orders the
kernels identically for both sweeps: an identical \(8\times\) miss reduction buys
little where there is little stall time to recover. The relation is
quantitative. Let \(f\) be \(f_{\text{stall}}\) at the 64-byte baseline. If
misses scale inversely with line size (as measured) and per-miss latency is
constant, stall time is \(2f\) at 32 bytes and \(f/4\) at 256 bytes relative to
baseline cycles, while non-stall time is unchanged. The 32-to-256-byte speedup
is then Amdahl's law with stalled fraction \(f_{32} = 2f/(1+f)\) and an
\(8\times\) speedup of that fraction:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{speedup}_{32\to256} = \frac{1}{(1 - f_{32}) + f_{32}/8}
	= \frac{1 + f}{1 - 0.75\,f}.
	\label{eq:amdahl}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} compares this prediction, from the baseline run
alone, with the measured speedups: the error is at most 0.3~percentage points for
four kernels and 1.4 points for \textsc{ata}x, in every case a slight
over-prediction, whose cause Section~\ref{sub:cacheline} identifies.

\subsection{Cache Line Size Effects}\label{sub:cacheline}

Line size acts through the same geometric mechanism in all five kernels: a wider
line amortizes one refill over more contiguous words, so for sequential access
the miss count falls in inverse proportion to line width; hence the near-exact
\(8.0\times\) reduction in L1D \textsc{mpki} from 32 to 256 bytes
(Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_line}) in every kernel but \textsc{ata}x
(\(7.6\times\)). What differs is how much that response is worth, and where the
misses are served sets the price: at baseline, Floyd-Warshall's misses hit in L2
(mean L1D miss latency 26 cycles), Jacobi-2D's and \textsc{gemm}'s in L3 (80
cycles), Seidel-2D's in L3 for reads and L2 for write upgrades (46 cycles), and
only \textsc{ata}x's reach \textsc{dram} (112 cycles). Two conditions govern the
value.

The first is that the access pattern be genuinely spatial rather than already
served by temporal reuse. \textsc{gemm} fails it despite a perfect \(8.0\times\)
miss reduction: row \(C_{i,:}\) is reused from L1D across the whole \(k\)-loop
and \(A_{i,k}\) is loop-invariant, so the only misses are the re-streaming of
\(B\) from L3 once per \(i\) (1.33~M L2 misses, within 1\% of \(N_I\) times the
6,600 lines of \(B\)); only \(3.4\%\) of cycles are stall-exposed and the gain is
capped at \(+5.8\%\). \textsc{ata}x is the opposite: it streams a 30.4~MiB matrix
with no reuse beyond the second pass over each row, every line of \(A\) is
fetched from \textsc{dram} once (L2 misses match \(A\)'s line count to 0.2\%),
and the miss reduction converts into \(+15.4\%\). Jacobi-2D falls between: each
source-grid line misses once per sweep (from L3, as 0.95~MiB exceeds L2), the
\(\pm N\) neighbors hit in L1D, and it gains \(+6.6\%\).

The second is that the working set be large enough, relative to the hierarchy,
for the kernel to be miss-bound at all. Floyd-Warshall fails it decisively: it
achieves the full \(8.0\times\) miss reduction, yet \textsc{ipc} moves only
\(+0.5\%\), because at \texttt{SMALL\_\-DATA\-SET} its 127~KiB footprint resides
in the 256~KiB L2, each miss costs 26 cycles, and \(0.4\%\) of cycles are
stall-exposed---an artifact of dataset reduction, not of the algorithm.
Seidel-2D's summed miss latency drops \(87.5\%\), yet it gains only \(+2.2\%\):
its \(f_{\text{stall}}\) is \(0.014\), so a large relative change in a memory
metric that governed little of execution means little.

Miss latency also explains the one residual of Eq.~\ref{eq:amdahl}. For the four
kernels served from L2 or L3, mean per-miss latency is identical at every line
size and the prediction holds to 0.3 points. For \textsc{ata}x it is 115, 112,
and 117 cycles at 32, 64, and 128 bytes but 159 at 256: a 256-byte line is four
64-byte \textsc{ddr3} bursts and eight beats on the 32-byte crossbars, so
transfer time, negligible at 64 bytes, becomes a third of the miss cost---the
bandwidth penalty of long lines noted by \cite{smith:1987}, and why \textsc{ata}x
gains \(15.4\%\) rather than the predicted \(16.8\%\). \textsc{dram} traffic is
invariant at 38.5~MiB (32.0 read, 8.4 written back), since \textsc{ata}x's misses
are compulsory: longer lines fetch the same bytes in fewer, larger transfers; the
other four kernels move under 4~KiB to \textsc{dram}.

\subsection{Associativity: A Structurally Low-Leverage Parameter}\label{sub:assoc}

Associativity's effect is not merely small but structurally bounded for these
kernels: it addresses conflict misses specifically, and the five kernels access
memory at unit stride, or at a fixed row offset for the stencils, so successive
addresses spread uniformly over the index bits and few simultaneously-live
addresses alias to one set. Their misses are compulsory and capacity misses,
which a parameter targeting conflict misses cannot help.

The data bear this out with almost no residual. At L1, \textsc{ipc} varies by at
most \(0.18\%\) (\textsc{ata}x) and by \(0.00\%\) for three kernels across the
entire 2-to-16-way range; L2 and L3 are flatter still. The sole exception is the
direct-mapped case, where every alias necessarily evicts: a direct-mapped L1D
has a miss rate up to \(96\%\) higher than an 8-way one (Floyd-Warshall), yet
even this is largely invisible in \textsc{ipc}, since eliminating it is worth
only \(+0.29\%\). This is the limiting case of \cite{hill:1989}: on general-purpose
traces each doubling beyond two ways still paid a diminishing return, whereas for
regular strides the second way captures essentially all the conflict pressure.
One curiosity runs the other way: \textsc{gemm}'s direct-mapped L2 is \(0.5\%\)
\textit{faster} than the 8-way \textsc{lru} one, because cyclic streaming of
\(B\) (422~KiB) through the 256~KiB L2 leaves \textsc{lru} nothing (5.5~K hits)
while direct mapping keeps a fixed subset (314~K hits).

\subsection{Capacity: A Threshold Effect, Not a Continuum}\label{sub:capacity}

Capacity is neither uniform (like line size) nor inert (like associativity) but a
\textit{threshold} effect, active only when the working set crosses the
\textsc{llc} boundary, as the agreement between Table~\ref{tab:workloads} and the
capacity column of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} shows. \textsc{ata}x is the only
kernel whose footprint (30.5~MiB, \(3.8\times\) the baseline \textsc{llc}) exceeds
the hierarchy, and the only one with a substantial response: \(+5.9\%\) for the
Ryzen~5600X and 7700X presets (32~MiB L3) and \(+7.2\%\) for Large Server (64~MiB
L3), precisely the configurations whose \textsc{llc} holds a meaningful fraction
of \(A\). The other four, at \(0.12\)--\(0.15\times\) the baseline \textsc{llc},
gain at most \(+2.7\%\), because they already fit.

The effect is non-monotonic in nominal capacity: for \textsc{ata}x the i9-9900K
preset (16~MiB L3) yields no gain while the Ryzen~5600X (32~MiB) yields
\(+5.9\%\), because what matters is whether the \textsc{llc} crosses the kernel's
reuse-distance threshold, not total bytes. The one negative result runs in the
expected direction: Seidel-2D loses \(3.1\%\) under Small Embedded, the only
preset shrinking the hierarchy, as its 1.22~MiB grid exceeds that preset's 1~MiB
L3. Because datasets were reduced for tractability, four of five kernels sit on
the insensitive side of the threshold, so our capacity effects are lower bounds
and part of the cross-kernel variation in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reflects
dataset choice: sensitivity is a property of the pair (kernel, dataset), acting
through \(f_{\text{stall}}\). One further null result: the L1I never exceeded 44
misses in any run, as the kernel's few kilobytes of instructions are resident
before \textit{libm5} starts collecting---a pitfall of \textsc{roi}-scoped
measurement, not evidence that instruction fetch is unimportant.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

For the five kernels analyzed, a single measured quantity decides which cache
parameter is worth tuning. All observed line-size and capacity sensitivity
follows from whether a kernel's access pattern is genuinely spatial rather than
absorbed by temporal reuse, and whether its working set is large enough for it
to be miss-bound at all. Both reduce to \(f_{\text{stall}}\), which orders the
kernels for both factors and, through Eq.~\ref{eq:amdahl}, predicts the
line-size speedup from one baseline run to within 1.4 points, the residual being
the transfer-time growth of \textsc{dram}-bound misses. Associativity is
structurally low-leverage for these kernels, whose regular strides rarely produce
the conflict misses it targets.

Several limitations bound these claims, and we do not generalize beyond them.
The unpipelined, blocking core is deliberately simplified: it neither overlaps
miss latency as out-of-order processors do nor pipelines hit latency as in-order
ones do, so absolute \(f_{\text{stall}}\) values and sensitivities are specific
to it, and Eq.~\ref{eq:amdahl} is untested where stalls overlap. Prefetching is
disabled, and on real hardware would recover part of the spatial locality that
line size exploits here. The workloads are regular polyhedral kernels at reduced
datasets, which says nothing about irregular or pointer-chasing applications,
where conflict misses and associativity may matter. Future work on the same
pipeline can address these: larger datasets, irregular applications,
prefetchers, replacement policies, and pipelined and out-of-order cores.
