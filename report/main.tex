\begin{abstract}

	Cache hierarchy parameters interact, so their individual payoffs are hard to
	rank from studies that vary several at once. We evaluate five PolyBench
	kernels under gem5, sweeping capacity, line size, and associativity one
	factor at a time from a common baseline, so that every observed change is
	attributable to a single parameter. Line size dominates: it cuts L1D misses
	almost identically across kernels, yet buys speedups ranging from 0.5\% to
	15.4\%, because what a kernel gains depends on how much of
	its execution was stalled on memory to begin with. Associativity proves
	structurally low-leverage for these regular access patterns and matters only
	in the degenerate direct-mapped case; capacity is a threshold effect
	that helps only where the hot working set exceeds the last-level cache. One
	measurable quantity, the fraction of execution exposed to L1D miss latency,
	orders the kernels by their response to both line size and capacity and
	predicts the line-size speedups through an Amdahl-style model, which thus
	supplies a transferable criterion for cache tuning. The whole pipeline is
	encoded as a single version-pinned build graph, so the methodology itself is
	reproducible.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The growing disparity between processor and main memory speeds, the
\textit{memory wall} identified three decades ago by Wulf and
McKee~\cite{wulf:1995}, remains the dominant first-order constraint on
single-thread performance. Every serious implementation of a modern instruction
set architecture (\textsc{isa}) mitigates it with a deep, multi-level cache
hierarchy that exploits temporal and spatial locality. The parameters of that
hierarchy---per-level capacity, block size, associativity, replacement policy,
and latency---are therefore among the most consequential decisions in
architecture design: they bound the achievable throughput of high-performance
parts and, equally, the energy budget and feasibility of low-power designs such
as single-board computers and embedded accelerators. Crucially, these
parameters are neither independent nor uniformly important. Their payoff is
workload-dependent, and a designer's practical question is not whether
a larger or more associative cache helps, but which parameter, at a fixed
transistor and energy budget, buys the most performance for a given class of
access patterns. Answering that question demands controlled empirical
exploration, since analytical reasoning alone cannot rank effects that interact
through miss classification, memory-level parallelism, and compute intensity.

The literature offers abundant evidence on individual points of this design
space, but comparatively little that lets a practitioner rank these parameters.
Architectural proposals typically fix the hierarchy and vary a single
dimension---for example, Khoshavi and DeMara~\cite{khoshavi:2018} substitute
the memory technology of the last-level cache---so the reported gains are
inseparable from the specific baseline configuration. Design-space exploration
studies such as Shahid et~al.~\cite{shahid:2025} sweep many parameters, but
target a single core and report aggregate performance rather than the per-level
miss behavior that explains \textit{why} a parameter matters. Methodological
work is even more pointed: Bueno et~al.~\cite{bueno:2024} show that
conclusions about cache replacement can shift by more than 30\% depending
solely on how simulation intervals are weighted, which implies that any cache
result is only as trustworthy as the procedure that produced it. Yet such
procedures are seldom shipped in executable form; artifacts, when available,
are usually loose scripts with unpinned simulator revisions and toolchains, so
a third party cannot regenerate the numbers---let alone extend the
sweep---without re-deriving the setup. The gap we address is thus twofold: an
absence of \textit{cross-workload, multi-parameter} cache sensitivity data
collected under one consistent methodology, and the absence of a
\textit{mechanized} pipeline in which that methodology is the artifact rather
than an appendix to it.

This paper closes that gap with a factor-isolating cache sensitivity study
whose entire experimental pipeline is encoded as a single, version-pinned build
graph executable by one command. We vary one factor at a time from a common
baseline: 11 multi-level capacity presets derived from shipping commercial
\textsc{cpu}s, cache line sizes from 32 to 256 bytes, and per-level
associativities from direct-mapped to 16-way, applied independently to L1, L2,
and L3. This grid runs over five PolyBench~4.2.1 kernels~\cite{polybench:2016}
spanning distinct locality regimes: dense streaming (\textsc{ata}x,
\textsc{gemm}), dynamic programming with structured reuse (Floyd-Warshall), and
iterative stencils (Jacobi-2D, Seidel-2D), for a total of 155
gem5~\cite{gem5-20:2007} simulations, each instrumented through gem5's
\textit{libm5} \textsc{api} so that statistics cover exactly the region of
interest.

Three results emerge, and their relative magnitudes are the substance of our
contribution. First, cache line size dominates, and does so uniformly at the
cache level: L1D \textsc{mpki} falls by almost exactly \(8.0\times\) from 32-
to 256-byte lines in four of five kernels, the geometric maximum for sequential
access. Yet the resulting \textsc{ipc} gains span \(+0.5\%\) to \(+15.4\%\),
because what differs between kernels is not the cache's response but how much
of their execution was exposed to it. Second, associativity is nearly inert:
\textsc{ipc} varies under \(0.2\%\) from 2- to 16-way, and only the
direct-mapped case is distinguishable. Third, capacity is a threshold effect
worth \(+7.2\%\) on \textsc{ata}x and at most \(+2.7\%\) elsewhere, activating
only where the working set exceeds the \textsc{llc}. Crucially, a single
measurable quantity, the fraction of baseline execution exposed to L1D miss
latency, ranks all five kernels correctly for both line size and capacity, and
predicts the line-size speedups within 1.8~percentage points via an
Amdahl-style model. This converts three separate empirical findings into one
mechanism observed at five exposures, and inverts the intuitive ordering of
cache parameters for regular numerical workloads: most performance gains
exploit spatial locality, not conflict avoidance or raw capacity.

Beyond the measurements themselves, this work contributes:
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\roman*), itemjoin={{; }}, itemjoin*={{; and }}]
	\item an explicit experimental design for cache attribution, isolating a
	categorical capacity factor from two numeric factors and holding the core
	model, \textsc{roi}, dataset, and toolchain fixed across the grid, so that
	every delta is attributable to a single parameter and comparable across
	kernels and levels
	\item a predictive criterion rather than just a ranking, reducing the
	observed sensitivities to two necessary conditions on a kernel
	(access-pattern spatiality and working-set size relative to the hierarchy),
	showing both are captured by one measurable quantity, and validating that
	quantity as an Amdahl-style predictor, so that a designer can estimate a new
	kernel's cache sensitivity from a single baseline run instead of a sweep
	\item a mechanically reproducible artifact in which the methodology
	\textit{is} the deliverable, the full workflow---dependency checks, a pinned
	Python interpreter and frozen packages, gem5 built at a recorded commit,
	static musl-linked workloads, the sweep, figure generation, and this
	manuscript---being a single idempotent build graph~\cite{repo:2026} whose
	runner is resumable and core-pinnable, and re-derives every figure and number
	here from raw \texttt{stats.txt} output with no manual step
	\item an extensible design-space harness, in which kernels, presets, and
	swept parameters are declarative inputs rather than hard-coded cases, so
	adding a workload or a new dimension (replacement policy, prefetcher,
	out-of-order core) reuses the same collection and analysis path
	\item documented null and negative results, which are usually discarded:
	associativity is inert above direct-mapped, most capacity presets are
	indistinguishable from baseline, and L1I traffic is effectively free (at most
	78 misses across all 155 runs)
\end{enumerate*}.
We explain each non-effect mechanically; together they delimit where tuning
effort is wasted, and the last exposes a measurement pitfall in
\textsc{roi}-scoped instruction-cache statistics.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

From an architectural optimization perspective, Khoshavi and
DeMara~\cite{khoshavi:2018} investigate emerging non-volatile memories in the
cache hierarchy, proposing \textsc{rrap} (Read Reference Activity Persistent), a
hybrid architecture replacing conventional \textsc{sram} with low- and
high-retention \textsc{stt}-\textsc{ram}. They report a 51.7\% reduction in L2
read miss rate alongside area and energy improvements. The gain is substantial,
but---as is typical of technology-substitution studies---it is measured against
one fixed hierarchy, so it cannot be compared against what conventional
parameter tuning would have achieved on the same workloads.

From a methodological standpoint, Bueno et~al.~\cite{bueno:2024} propose a
weight redefinition methodology for simulation intervals based on memory
activity (\textsc{mpki}), reducing discrepancies in replacement-policy analysis
by more than 30\%. We read this as the strongest available argument that cache
conclusions are procedure-dependent, and it directly motivates our decision to
ship the procedure as an executable artifact.

Validation in this domain relies on established infrastructure: gem5
\cite{gem5-20:2007} for detailed modeling and PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}
for representative kernels with well-characterized locality. gem5's flexibility
has driven work on Design Space Exploration (\textsc{dse}), of which Shahid
et~al.~\cite{shahid:2025} is representative, using parametric simulation to
balance hardware cost and performance in the \textsc{risc-v} \textsc{cva6} core.
Such studies establish gem5's suitability for \textsc{dse}; our work differs in
sweeping factors orthogonally to attribute effects per parameter, and in
reporting the per-level miss behavior that explains them, rather than optimizing
one core's aggregate performance.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
\subsection{Design Rationale}\label{sub:rationale}

Every element of the experimental setup follows from the requirement that any
observed change in a metric be attributable to exactly one cache parameter. We
state the resulting choices and their justifications together below.

\subsubsection{One factor at a time}
We sweep capacity, line size, and associativity, but never jointly. Varying them
together reproduces the confound identified in Section~\ref{sec:related}: when
bundled commercial configurations are compared, a difference cannot be ascribed
to any one dimension, since presets differ in capacity \textit{and} line size
\textit{and} associativity at once. Sweeping one factor from a fixed baseline
costs more simulations (155 rather than a handful) but is the only route to
per-parameter attribution, and is what lets the associativity result be reported
as a property of the workload class rather than of one preset.

\subsubsection{In-order core (\textit{TimingSimpleCPU})}
We deliberately choose the simplest core model that still charges realistic
memory timing. An out-of-order core overlaps miss latency with independent
work, so an identical reduction in misses produces a smaller and
\textit{configuration-dependent} change in \textsc{ipc}. That is a legitimate
object of study, but it entangles the cache effect we want to measure with the
core's ability to hide it. With an in-order model, a miss that is not serviced
stalls the pipeline, so \textsc{cpi} responds to cache behavior almost
linearly, and the resulting sensitivities are upper bounds that isolate the
cache. We likewise disable prefetching: a prefetcher partially substitutes for
line size by fetching ahead, which would mask precisely the spatial-locality
effect under investigation. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} states the
consequences of this choice for the generality of our conclusions.

\subsubsection{Capacity as realistic presets rather than a free sweep} We use
11 multi-level presets derived from shipping commercial \textsc{cpu}s
(Table~\ref{tbl:cachesizes}) because per-level capacities are, in practice,
co-designed under area and latency constraints, not independently chosen. The
presets thus span a range a practitioner might actually face, from a small
embedded part to a large server. The cost is that they are
\textit{categorical}, varying several levels at once, so we treat them as one
factor and report deltas against a baseline, never attributing a preset's
effect to a specific level. The Intel®~Core™~i7-6700K is the baseline for all
other sweeps.\footnote{For the Intel Atom and IBM POWER10 configurations, cache
sizes were adjusted to the nearest power-of-two values to ensure compatibility
with the gem5 simulator. Apple Silicon cache sizes are taken from the
energy-efficiency cores.}

\subsubsection{Line size and associativity ranges} Line size is swept over four
power-of-two values from 32 to 256 bytes around a 64-byte baseline, bracketing
where real designs sit and extending one step beyond in each direction to
expose the trend rather than a single point. Associativity is swept over 1, 2,
4, 8, and 16-way independently for L1, L2, and L3 (baselines 8-way for L1/L2,
16-way for L3), since varying one level at a time localizes conflict pressure
to where it arises.\footnote{A 12-way associativity configuration would be
desirable, as it exists in the Zen~5 architecture, but gem5 expects
power-of-two associativities.} Including the direct-mapped case is essential
rather than pro~forma: it is the only configuration in which every set conflict
necessarily evicts, and proves to be the sole associativity setting with a
measurable effect.

\subsubsection{Kernel selection}
The five kernels place in different combinations the two mechanisms we
ultimately identify as decisive, namely access-pattern spatiality and
working-set size relative to the hierarchy, rather than covering application
domains. \textsc{ata}x contributes a large streaming working set;
\textsc{gemm}, strong temporal reuse at a small footprint; Floyd-Warshall,
heavy reuse over a footprint that fits entirely in L2; and Jacobi-2D and
Seidel-2D, structured stencil locality, the latter with loop-carried
dependencies the former lacks. The suite therefore contains kernels satisfying
neither, one, or both conditions, which is what makes the cross-workload
comparison informative.

\subsubsection{Reduced datasets}
Dataset sizes are reduced per kernel (Section~\ref{sub:dataset-size}) because
detailed timing simulation at the default \texttt{LARGE\_DATASET} would take
prohibitively long across 155 runs. The trade-off is consequential: smaller
footprints reduce pressure on the hierarchy, compressing the capacity effects
we measure and, as Section~\ref{sub:capacity} shows, accounting for
Floyd-Warshall's near-total insensitivity. We therefore treat dataset size as
an experimental variable reported alongside the results, not an implementation
detail.

\subsubsection{Simulator choice}
We use gem5~\cite{gem5-20:2007} rather than a faster interval-based simulator
such as Sniper because our quantities of interest are detailed per-level miss
counts and latencies under a fully event-driven cache and interconnect model.
While Sniper provides CPI stacks and hierarchy statistics, its approximate
interval-based timing model prioritizes simulation speed over the fidelity we
require for decomposing the effects we study.

\subsection{Simulated System}

The simulated gem5 system uses a \textit{TimingSimpleCPU} with the x86
\textsc{isa} (an in-order model with memory access timing, justified in
Section~\ref{sub:rationale}), operating at 4~GHz over a physical address space
of 8~GiB. The processor is connected to a multi-level cache hierarchy
consisting of private L1 instruction and data caches, followed by shared L2 and
L3 caches. Each cache level is equipped with a finite number of Miss Status
Holding Registers (\textsc{mshr}s), which enable multiple outstanding memory
requests and thus admit limited memory-level parallelism even under an in-order
core. Communication between cache levels and main memory is modeled using a
hierarchical crossbar interconnect, with dedicated crossbars between each level
and a data width of 32 bytes. Because the simulator's clock period is 250~ticks
at this frequency, all latency figures reported in ticks can be converted to
cycles by dividing by 250. The complete system diagram, as automatically
generated by gem5 for each run, is included in the artifact
repository~\cite{repo:2026}.

\subsection{Workload Selection}\label{sub:workloads}

We use five PolyBench Version~4.2.1~\cite{polybench:2016} kernels:
\textsc{ata}x, Floyd-Warshall, \textsc{gemm}, Jacobi-2D, and Seidel-2D. These
kernels capture a range of memory access patterns, including dense linear
algebra with regular streaming accesses (\textsc{ata}x, \textsc{gemm}), dynamic
programming with structured temporal reuse (Floyd-Warshall), and iterative
stencil computations with localized spatial reuse (Jacobi-2D, Seidel-2D); the
selection criterion is stated in Section~\ref{sub:rationale}.
Table~\ref{tab:workingsets} gives the resulting working sets, which prove to be
the decisive quantity in Section~\ref{sec:results}. All kernels use
double-precision data except Floyd-Warshall, whose PolyBench definition uses
\texttt{int}.

\subsubsection{\textsc{gemm} (General Matrix Multiply)} The \textsc{gemm}
kernel implements dense matrix multiplication, a core operation in
\textsc{blas} (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms)~\cite{lawson:1979}, computing
a linear combination of matrix products:

\[
C_{ij} \leftarrow \beta C_{ij} + \alpha \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} A_{ik} B_{kj}
\]

From PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}:
\[
\text{Time: } O(n^3), \quad \text{Memory: } O(n^2)
\]

This kernel exhibits regular, dense access patterns with high spatial locality
and significant temporal reuse, particularly in the accumulation of $C_{ij}$.

\subsubsection{\textsc{ata}x (Matrix-Vector Transpose Product)} The
\textsc{ata}x kernel computes a matrix-vector product followed by a transpose
multiplication, a common pattern in iterative solvers.

\[
y = A^T (A x)
\]

From PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}:
\[
\text{Time: } O(n^2), \quad \text{Memory: } O(n^2)
\]

The kernel combines row-major streaming accesses in the forward multiplication
with column-wise accesses in the transpose phase, which results in mixed
spatial locality and limited temporal reuse.

\subsubsection{Floyd-Warshall (All-Pairs Shortest Paths)} The Floyd-Warshall
algorithm computes shortest paths between all pairs of vertices in a weighted
graph using dynamic programming~\cite{floyd:1962, warshall:1962}.

\[
D_{ij}^{(k)} = \min\left(D_{ij}^{(k-1)},\; D_{ik}^{(k-1)} + D_{kj}^{(k-1)}\right)
\]

From PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}:
\[
\text{Time: } O(n^3), \quad \text{Memory: } O(n^2)
\]

The algorithm performs dense, regular accesses over a 2D matrix with strong
temporal reuse across iterations of the outer loop.

\subsubsection{Jacobi-2D (Iterative Stencil Method)} The Jacobi-2D kernel
applies an iterative stencil computation over a 2D grid, commonly used in
numerical solutions of partial differential equations~\cite{golub:2013}.

\[
A_{i,j}^{(t+1)} =
\frac{1}{5}\left(
A_{i,j}^{(t)} +
A_{i-1,j}^{(t)} +
A_{i+1,j}^{(t)} +
A_{i,j-1}^{(t)} +
A_{i,j+1}^{(t)}
\right)
\]

From PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}:
\[
\text{Time: } O(n^3), \quad \text{Memory: } O(n^2)
\]

The kernel exhibits structured spatial locality due to stencil-based neighbor
accesses, with moderate temporal locality across time steps.

\subsubsection{Seidel-2D (Gauss--Seidel Stencil)} The Seidel-2D kernel
implements an in-place stencil computation using a Gauss-Seidel update scheme,
where newly computed values are immediately reused within the same iteration.

\[
A_{i,j} \leftarrow \frac{1}{9}
\sum_{p=-1}^{1} \sum_{q=-1}^{1} A_{i+p,j+q}
\]

From PolyBench~\cite{polybench:2016}:
\[
\text{Time: } O(n^3), \quad \text{Memory: } O(n^2)
\]

Compared to Jacobi-2D, the in-place update introduces loop-carried
dependencies, increasing temporal locality but reducing
parallelism~\cite{golub:2013}.

\subsection{Collection of Metrics}

Each workload was compiled as a statically linked 64-bit \textsc{elf} executable
targeting the x86-64 \textsc{isa}, against the musl C standard
library~\cite{musl} to ensure minimal and deterministic runtime dependencies.
Binaries were linked against gem5's \textit{libm5} library, whose \textsc{api}
we use to delimit the region of interest (\textsc{roi}): annotation calls are
placed immediately before and after the kernel invocation, so statistics cover
the kernel and exclude initialization and output.

For each run we record from gem5's \texttt{stats.txt}: throughput
(\textsc{ipc}/\textsc{cpi}); per-level hits, misses, and miss rates; and two
derived quantities, per-level \textsc{mpki} (misses per kilo instructions) and
total miss latency (summed across levels) as a proxy for memory stalls. We also
derive \(f_{\text{stall}}\), the fraction of baseline execution cycles exposed
to L1D miss latency, which Section~\ref{sec:results} shows to be the decisive
predictor. Categorical presets are reported as deltas or speedup against a
per-workload baseline
(\(\mathrm{\textsc{ipc}}/\mathrm{\textsc{ipc}}_{\text{baseline}}\)), while
numeric sweeps are plotted as absolute values, with logarithmic scales where
needed to span orders of magnitude.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Build Pipeline}

We express the end-to-end workflow as an ordered, idempotent build graph in the
Zig Build System (tied to Zig version~0.15.2~\cite{zig:2024}), encoding in
dependency order: dependency checks; a pinned Python runtime via \texttt{uv} and
a project virtual environment; gem5 submodule initialization and compilation;
the \textit{libm5} library build; workload compilation and linking; the
simulation sweep; figure generation; and the final \LaTeX{} report build.

Versions are pinned to minimize drift: Python is fixed to 3.14.3, all packages
are frozen in \texttt{requirements.txt}, and gem5 is vendored as a submodule
built at an exact recorded commit (\texttt{7a2b0e4}). The only component not
strictly pinned is the C toolchain; we use GCC~\(\geq 14\) and observe no
material effect on comparative cache trends within that range. Simulations are
dispatched by a Python orchestrator that enumerates the grid, manages a worker
pool, optionally pins workers to physical cores to reduce noise, and writes
metrics under deterministic paths; completion markers and key-counter checks
make the sweep idempotent and resumable. All source and artifacts are publicly
available~\cite{repo:2026}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Overview and Ranking of the Three Factors}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} summarizes the entire sweep in the form that matters
for design decisions: how much \textsc{ipc} each factor buys, per kernel. Three
observations organize this section. First, the factors are separated by more than
an order of magnitude in leverage: line size moves \textsc{ipc} by up to
\(15.4\%\), capacity by up to \(7.2\%\), associativity (above direct-mapped) by
at most \(0.2\%\). Second, the kernel ordering is the same for line size
and capacity, suggesting one underlying quantity governs both. Third, the
mechanism is uniform where the outcome is not: L1D \textsc{mpki} falls almost
exactly \(8.0\times\) for four of five kernels, yet \textsc{ipc} gains span
\(0.5\%\) to \(15.4\%\). The cache behaves identically; what differs is how much
of each kernel's execution was exposed to it.

A single measured quantity accounts for this: \(f_{\text{stall}}\), the fraction
of a kernel's baseline cycles spent stalled on L1D misses (last column of
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). It ranges from \(0.004\) for Floyd-Warshall to
\(0.090\) for \textsc{ata}x, a 25-fold spread, and it orders the kernels
identically for both the line-size and the capacity sweeps. This is why an
identical \(8.0\times\) reduction in misses buys so little for one kernel and so
much for another: there is simply far more stall time to recover in
\textsc{ata}x. Treating the line-size sweep as Amdahl's law over the stalled
fraction, \(\mathrm{speedup} = 1/(1 - k\,f_{\text{stall}})\) with \(k\) the
fraction of misses eliminated (\(\approx0.875\)), predicts every measured
speedup to within two percentage points. Each per-kernel result that follows is
the same mechanism observed at a different value of \(f_{\text{stall}}\).

\subsection{Cache Line Size Effects}\label{sub:cacheline}

Line size acts through the same essentially geometric mechanism in all five
kernels: a wider line amortizes one tag lookup and one refill over more
contiguous words, so for sequential access the miss count falls in inverse
proportion to line width. This appears as a near-exact \(8.0\times\) reduction in
L1D \textsc{mpki} from 32 to 256 bytes (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison_line}) in every
kernel but \textsc{ata}x, where it is \(7.6\times\). The cache thus responds
almost identically across kernels; what differs is how much that response is
worth, and two independent conditions govern its value, either of which a kernel
may fail on its own.

The first condition is that the access pattern be genuinely spatial rather than
already served by temporal reuse. \textsc{gemm} fails it despite a perfect
\(8.0\times\) miss reduction: its inner loop reuses the \(C_{ij}\) accumulator
across the entire \(k\)-reduction, so the accesses dominating its execution are
already L1D hits regardless of line width. Only \(3.4\%\) of its baseline cycles
are stall-exposed, capping the gain at \(+5.8\%\) (\textsc{cpi} \(7.14 \text{ to }
6.75\), Fig.~\ref{fig:line-composite}d). \textsc{ata}x is the opposite: its forward
phase streams a contiguous \(1900\times2100\) matrix with almost no reuse, so
nearly every access is a candidate for spatial amortization, converting the miss
reduction into \(+15.4\%\) \textsc{ipc} (Fig.~\ref{fig:line-composite}a). The
stencils fall between: Jacobi-2D touches neighbors at offsets \(\pm 1\) and
\(\pm N\), so only the former is contiguous, yielding \(+6.6\%\).

The second condition is that the working set be large enough, relative to the
hierarchy, for the kernel to be miss-bound at all. Floyd-Warshall fails it
decisively and is the clearest negative result in the sweep: it achieves the full
\(8.0\times\) miss reduction and its \(O(n^3)\) recurrence reuses the \(path\)
matrix heavily, yet \textsc{ipc} moves only \(+0.5\%\). At
\texttt{SMALL\_DATASET} its entire 127~KiB footprint
(Table~\ref{tab:workingsets}) already resides in the 256~KiB baseline L2, so just
\(0.4\%\) of its cycles are stall-exposed---nothing remains for a spatial
optimization to recover. This is an artifact of our dataset reduction, not a
property of the algorithm.

Seidel-2D demonstrates why absolute latency reductions must not be read as
performance. It shows one of the largest absolute drops in total miss latency in
the suite, \(162\times10^9 \text{ to } 20\times10^9\) ticks (\(-87.5\%\),
Fig.~\ref{fig:line-composite}c), yet gains only \(+2.2\%\) \textsc{ipc}. Two
effects compound: \(f_{\text{stall}}\) is just \(0.014\), since the kernel
executes \(2.0\times10^9\) instructions over a 1.22~MiB grid and is
overwhelmingly compute-bound; and its in-place update carries loop-carried
dependencies, so removing memory stalls exposes a dependency bound rather than
releasing issue slots. A large relative change in a memory metric is compatible
with negligible performance change whenever that metric governed a small share
of execution time.

The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} is thus fully explained by joint
satisfaction of the two conditions, and no kernel failing either was rescued by a
larger line, nor did any satisfying both fail to benefit---which is what licenses
treating them as necessary conditions rather than correlates.

\subsection{Associativity: A Structurally Low-Leverage Parameter}\label{sub:assoc}

Associativity is the one factor whose effect is not merely small but also
structurally bounded for this workload class, and the reason is worth
stating precisely because it generalizes beyond our five kernels. Associativity
addresses conflict misses specifically: it lets several lines that index to the
same set coexist. Our kernels, however, access memory at unit stride or at a
fixed offset of \(N\) for the stencils, so successive addresses distribute
roughly uniformly over the index bits, and the number of distinct
simultaneously-live addresses aliasing to any single set stays small. Their
misses are consequently dominated by compulsory and capacity effects, against
which extra ways are powerless. A parameter that targets a miss category the
workload barely generates cannot help, however generously it is provisioned.

The data bear this out with almost no residual: at L1, \textsc{ipc} varies by at
most \(0.18\%\) (\textsc{ata}x), and by \(0.00\%\) for Jacobi-2D and Seidel-2D,
across the entire 2-to-16-way range (Fig.~\ref{fig:comp-ipc-l1}). Deeper levels
are flatter still: over the same range the L2 sweep moves \textsc{ipc} by
\(0.00\%\) in all five kernels, and the L3 sweep by at most \(0.0007\%\), which
is the expected result once L1 has already absorbed the conflict pressure. The
sole exception is the direct-mapped case, exceptional because with one way every
alias necessarily evicts, there being no alternative frame for the incoming
line. A direct-mapped L1D thus carries a higher miss rate than an 8-way one:
\(+96\%\) in Floyd-Warshall, \(+31\%\) in \textsc{gemm}, \(+19\%\) in Jacobi-2D,
\(+11\%\) in Seidel-2D, \(+3\%\) in \textsc{ata}x. Even these are largely
invisible in \textsc{ipc}---eliminating Floyd-Warshall's \(96\%\) penalty is
worth \(+0.29\%\)---the same lesson Seidel-2D taught for line size. A single
additional way captures essentially all the conflict pressure these kernels
produce; further associativity is wasted area.

\subsection{Capacity: A Threshold Effect, Not a Continuum}\label{sub:capacity}

Capacity behaves qualitatively differently from the other two factors: it is
neither uniform (like line size) nor inert (like associativity) but a
\textit{threshold} effect, active only when the working set crosses the
\textsc{llc} boundary. The evidence is the near-perfect agreement between
Table~\ref{tab:workingsets} and the capacity column of
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}. \textsc{ata}x is the only kernel whose footprint
(30.5~MiB, \(3.8\times\) the baseline \textsc{llc}) exceeds the hierarchy, and it
is the only kernel with a substantial response: \(+5.9\%\) for the Ryzen~5600X
and 7700X presets (32~MiB L3) and \(+7.2\%\) for Large Server (64~MiB L3),
these being precisely the configurations whose \textsc{llc} can hold a
meaningful fraction of matrix \(A\) (Figs.~\ref{fig:comp-ipc-size}
and~\ref{fig:atax-speed-size}). The remaining four kernels have footprints of
\(0.12\)--\(0.15\times\) the baseline \textsc{llc} and gain at most \(+2.7\%\),
because they already fit, and additional capacity buys nothing.

Two aspects are easy to misread. First, the effect is non-monotonic in nominal
capacity: for \textsc{ata}x the i9-9900K preset (16~MiB L3) yields no
gain while the Ryzen~5600X (32~MiB) yields \(+5.9\%\), because what matters is
not total bytes but whether the \textsc{llc} crosses the kernel's reuse-distance
threshold; ranking presets by aggregate size would mispredict performance.
Second, the one negative result runs in the expected direction: Seidel-2D loses
\(3.1\%\) \textsc{ipc} under Small Embedded, the only preset that
shrinks the hierarchy below baseline (16~KiB L1, 128~KiB L2, 1~MiB L3).
Its 1.22~MiB grid exceeds that 1~MiB L3, and the L3 miss rate jumps from
\(7\times10^{-6}\) to \(1.0\); that is, every L3 access misses. This is the
same threshold mechanism in reverse, confirming that the boundary, not the
magnitude, governs the effect.

The corollary for our methodology is worth stating plainly: because datasets were
reduced for tractability, four of five kernels sit on the insensitive side of this
threshold, so our capacity effects are lower bounds and Floyd-Warshall's flatness
is an artifact of \texttt{SMALL\_DATASET} rather than evidence about the
algorithm. Capacity remains secondary to line size for regular kernels, but our
data establish this only for working sets that fit the baseline hierarchy.

\subsection{Secondary Observations}

\subsubsection{Instruction cache traffic is free} Across all 155 simulations
the instruction cache never exceeded 78 misses (at most \(0.34\) \textsc{mpki};
the miss rate rounds to zero at gem5's precision in most runs). The cause is
the interaction between our \textsc{roi} instrumentation and code size: by the
time the binary calls \textit{libm5} to begin collecting statistics, the
kernel's instruction footprint, a few kilobytes, has already been fetched and
fits trivially in any L1I we simulate. L1I is therefore insensitive to every
swept parameter and we exclude it. We report this because it is a general
pitfall of \textsc{roi}-scoped measurement rather than a property of these
kernels: any study instrumenting a tight numerical loop will find its
instruction-side statistics pre-warmed and uninformative, and should not
conclude that instruction fetch is unimportant in the full application.

\subsubsection{Dataset size is a confound} Because dataset sizes
differ per kernel (Section~\ref{sub:dataset-size}), part of the cross-kernel
variation in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} is attributable to dataset choice rather
than intrinsic kernel behavior---most consequentially at the extremes, since
\textsc{ata}x is the only kernel at \texttt{LARGE\_DATASET} and shows the largest
response, while Floyd-Warshall is the only one at \texttt{SMALL\_DATASET} and
shows the smallest. We cannot fully separate the two explanations here. What we
can say is that the mechanism is identified: \(f_{\text{stall}}\) accounts for the
ordering, and dataset size acts on \(f_{\text{stall}}\) by changing the working
set relative to the hierarchy. Sensitivity is thus a property of the pair
(kernel, dataset), which is why we report footprints in
Table~\ref{tab:workingsets}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The chief product of this work is not the individual measurements reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, but a compact, transferable criterion for deciding
\textit{a priori} which cache parameter is worth tuning for a given regular
numerical kernel. Across five PolyBench kernels spanning dense linear algebra,
dynamic programming, and stencil computation, essentially all observed
line-size and capacity sensitivity is explained by whether a kernel satisfies
two conditions jointly: (i)~its access pattern is genuinely spatial rather than
already absorbed by temporal reuse or compute intensity, and (ii)~its working
set is large enough, relative to the cache hierarchy, that the kernel is
miss-bound to begin with. \textsc{ata}x satisfies both and is the only kernel to
respond strongly to both line size (\(+15.4\%\)) and capacity (\(+7.2\%\));
\textsc{gemm} fails~(i) through its \(C_{ij}\) reuse and Floyd-Warshall
fails~(ii) with a 127~KiB footprint inside a 256~KiB L2, and both are
correspondingly insensitive (\(+5.8\%\) and \(+0.5\%\)).

The criterion is operational: it lets a designer predict a kernel's cache
sensitivity, not just explain it after the fact. Both conditions reduce to one
measurable quantity, the fraction \(f_{\text{stall}}\) of execution exposed to
L1D miss latency, which ranks all five kernels correctly for both factors while
predicting line-size speedups to within 1.8~percentage points through an
Amdahl-style model. A designer facing a new regular kernel can therefore
estimate its cache sensitivity from a single baseline measurement rather than a
sweep. Associativity requires no such estimate: because it targets conflict
misses specifically, and regular strides generate few conflicts, it is
structurally low-leverage for this entire workload class. Stated this way, the
finding generalizes beyond \enquote{kernel X gained Y\%.}

A second contribution is methodological. Sweeping the three factors separately
around a common baseline, rather than jointly as happens implicitly when only
bundled commercial configurations are compared, is what makes per-parameter
attribution possible at all, and what permits the associativity result to be
identified as a general property of low-conflict regular kernels rather than a
coincidence of one preset.

Several limitations bound these claims. The in-order core does not capture
out-of-order execution or memory-level parallelism, so our sensitivities are
upper bounds and the Amdahl-style model---which assumes stalls are exposed
rather than overlapped---remains untested where they are not. Dataset reduction
leaves four of five kernels below the \textsc{llc} threshold, making our capacity
effects lower bounds and Floyd-Warshall's flatness an artifact of
\texttt{SMALL\_DATASET} rather than of the algorithm. The workloads are regular
by construction, so the criterion's applicability to irregular or
indirection-intensive applications remains open.

Reproducibility is part of the knowledge this work contributes: the entire
pipeline is a deterministic, idempotent build graph with pinned versions, so
the methodology can be re-run, audited, or extended at low marginal
cost~\cite{repo:2026}. Future work may exercise it to test the criterion
directly: scaling datasets so all kernels straddle the \textsc{llc} threshold,
adding irregular applications, sweeping replacement and prefetching policies,
and re-running the grid on an out-of-order core to measure how much of each
sensitivity survives latency hiding.
