\begin{abstract}

	Cache hierarchy parameters interact, so their individual payoffs are hard to
	rank. We evaluate five PolyBench kernels under gem5, sweeping capacity, line
	size, and associativity one factor at a time from a common baseline, so every
	change is attributable to a single parameter. Line size dominates: it cuts L1D
	misses almost identically across kernels, yet buys speedups from 0.5\% to
	15.4\%. Associativity is structurally low-leverage for regular accesses, and
	capacity is a threshold effect. One quantity, the fraction of execution exposed
	to L1D miss latency, orders the kernels for both factors and predicts the
	line-size speedups via an Amdahl-style model, giving a transferable tuning
	criterion.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The disparity between processor and main memory speeds---the \textit{memory wall}
identified three decades ago \cite{wulf:1995}---remains the dominant first-order
constraint on single-thread performance, and every modern instruction set
architecture (\textsc{isa}) implementation mitigates it with a deep, multi-level
cache hierarchy. Its parameters---per-level capacity, block size, associativity,
replacement policy, and latency---bound both the throughput of high-performance
parts and the energy budget of low-power designs, yet are neither independent nor
uniformly important. The designer's practical question is not whether a larger or
more associative cache helps, but which parameter, at a fixed transistor and energy
budget, buys the most performance for a given class of access patterns; which
analytical reasoning cannot settle, since the effects interact through miss
classification, memory-level parallelism, and compute intensity.

The literature offers abundant evidence on individual points of this design space
but little that lets a practitioner rank the parameters. Architectural proposals
fix the hierarchy and vary one dimension---\cite{khoshavi:2018}, for instance,
substitutes the memory technology of the last-level cache---so gains are
inseparable from the baseline. Design-space explorations such as
\cite{shahid:2025} sweep many parameters but target one core and report aggregate
performance, not the per-level miss behavior that explains \textit{why} a
parameter matters. And \cite{bueno:2024} shows that conclusions about cache
replacement shift by more than 30\% depending solely on interval weighting, so a
cache result is only as trustworthy as the procedure behind it---yet such
procedures are seldom shipped in executable form. The gap is thus twofold: no
\textit{cross-workload, multi-parameter} sensitivity data under one consistent
methodology, and no \textit{mechanized} pipeline in which that methodology is
itself the artifact.

We close it with a factor-isolating study whose entire pipeline is one
version-pinned build graph executable by a single command. Varying one factor at a
time from a common baseline, we sweep 11 multi-level capacity presets derived from
shipping commercial \textsc{cpu}s, line sizes from 32 to 256 bytes, and
associativities from direct-mapped to 16-way per level, over five PolyBench~4.2.1
kernels \cite{polybench:2016}: dense streaming (\textsc{ata}x, \textsc{gemm}),
dynamic programming (Floyd-Warshall), and iterative stencils (Jacobi-2D,
Seidel-2D). This totals 155 gem5 \cite{gem5-20:2007} simulations, each instrumented
through gem5's \textit{libm5} \textsc{api} so that statistics cover exactly the
region of interest (\textsc{roi}).

The three factors differ by more than an order of magnitude in leverage: line
size buys up to \(+15.4\%\) \textsc{ipc}, capacity up to \(+7.2\%\), and
associativity above direct-mapped at most \(0.2\%\); but the ranking matters
less than its explanation. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item an experimental design isolating each cache parameter, so every
	measured change is attributable to a single factor and comparable across
	kernels and levels;
	\item a predictive criterion: the two decisive mechanisms reduce to one
	measurable quantity, \(f_{\text{stall}}\), which orders the kernels for both
	factors and predicts the line-size speedups, so a new kernel's sensitivity
	follows from one baseline run rather than a sweep;
	\item a reproducible artifact in which the methodology \textit{is} the
	deliverable, as a single idempotent build graph \cite{repo:2026} re-derives
	every number here from gem5's raw output; and
	\item null results usually discarded: associativity is inert above
	direct-mapped, most capacity presets match baseline, and instruction-cache
	traffic is negligible.
\end{itemize}
The upshot inverts the intuitive ordering of cache parameters for regular
numerical workloads: most gains exploit spatial locality, not conflict avoidance
or raw capacity.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} presents related previous work.
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} details the experimental design, simulated system,
metrics, and workloads. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the sweep results and
the analysis that yields the criterion, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

From an architectural optimization perspective, \cite{khoshavi:2018} investigates
non-volatile memories in the cache hierarchy, proposing \textsc{rrap} (Read
Reference Activity Persistent), a hybrid architecture replacing conventional
\textsc{sram} with low- and high-retention \textsc{stt}-\textsc{ram}, and reports
a 51.7\% reduction in L2 read miss rate alongside area and energy improvements.
The gain is substantial, but, as is typical of technology-substitution
studies, it is measured against one fixed hierarchy, so it cannot be compared
against what conventional parameter tuning would have achieved. Methodologically,
\cite{bueno:2024} redefines simulation-interval weights from memory activity
(\textsc{mpki}), reducing discrepancies in replacement-policy analysis by more
than 30\%: the strongest available argument that cache conclusions are
procedure-dependent, and our motivation for shipping the procedure as an
executable artifact.

Validation relies on established infrastructure: we use gem5 for detailed
modeling \cite{gem5-20:2007} and PolyBench for kernels with well-characterized
locality \cite{polybench:2016}. gem5's flexibility has driven Design
Space Exploration (\textsc{dse}) work, of which \cite{shahid:2025} is
representative, balancing hardware cost and performance in the \textsc{risc-v}
\textsc{cva6} core. Such studies establish gem5's suitability for \textsc{dse};
ours differs in sweeping factors orthogonally to attribute effects per parameter,
and in reporting the per-level miss behavior that explains them.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
\subsection{Experimental Design}\label{sub:rationale}

Every element of the setup follows from the requirement that any observed change
be attributable to exactly one cache parameter. We therefore sweep the three
factors separately from a fixed baseline rather than jointly: comparing bundled
commercial configurations confounds them, since presets differ in capacity
\textit{and} line size \textit{and} associativity at once. This costs 155
simulations, but it is the only route to per-parameter attribution. Capacity is
varied as 11 multi-level presets derived from shipping commercial \textsc{cpu}s
(Table~\ref{tbl:cachesizes}), since per-level capacities are co-designed under
area and latency constraints and not chosen independently; being
\textit{categorical}, they are treated as one factor and reported as deltas
against the Intel®~Core™~i7-6700K baseline, never attributing an effect to a
specific level. Line size is swept over four power-of-two values from 32 to 256
bytes around a 64-byte baseline, and associativity over 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 ways
independently for L1, L2, and L3 (baselines 8-way for L1/L2, 16-way for L3),
since varying one level at a time localizes conflict pressure.\footnote{Gem5
expects powers of two, so the 12-way configuration of the Zen~5 \textsc{cpu}s
cannot be reproduced.} The direct-mapped case is included because it is the
only configuration in which every set conflict necessarily evicts, and proves
to be the sole associativity setting with a measurable effect.

Three further choices isolate the cache from the core. We use gem5's in-order
\textit{TimingSimpleCPU}, the simplest model that still charges realistic memory
timing: an out-of-order core overlaps miss latency with independent work, so an
identical reduction in misses yields a smaller, configuration-dependent
\textsc{ipc} change, entangling the cache effect with the core's ability to hide
it. Under an in-order model an unserviced miss stalls the pipeline and
\textsc{cpi} responds almost linearly, so our sensitivities are upper bounds that
isolate the cache. We disable prefetching, which partially substitutes for line
size and would mask the very effect under investigation, and we prefer gem5 to an
interval-based simulator such as Sniper because our quantities of interest are
per-level miss counts and latencies under a fully event-driven model. Finally,
dataset sizes are reduced per kernel (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) because detailed
timing simulation at the default \texttt{LARGE\_\-DATA\-SET} would be prohibitive
across 155 runs; this compresses the capacity effects we measure and accounts for
Floyd-Warshall's near-total insensitivity (Section~\ref{sub:capacity}), so we
treat dataset size as a reported experimental variable.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} states the consequences for generality.

\subsection{Simulated System and Metrics}

The simulated system pairs the in-order \textit{TimingSimpleCPU} with the x86
\textsc{isa} at 4~GHz over an 8~GiB physical address space. Private L1 instruction
and data caches are backed by shared L2 and L3 levels, each with a finite number of
Miss Status Holding Registers (\textsc{mshr}s), admitting limited memory-level
parallelism even under an in-order core; levels communicate through a hierarchical
crossbar of 32-byte data width, and the 250-tick clock period converts tick
latencies to cycles. Each workload was compiled as a statically linked 64-bit
\textsc{elf} for x86-64 against the musl C library \cite{musl} and linked against
gem5's \textit{libm5}, whose \textsc{api} delimits the \textsc{roi}: annotation
calls immediately before and after the kernel invocation confine statistics to the
kernel, excluding initialization and output.

From each run's \texttt{stats.txt} we record throughput
(\textsc{ipc}/\textsc{cpi}), per-level hits, misses and miss rates, per-level
\textsc{mpki} (misses per kilo instructions), total miss latency summed across
levels as a proxy for memory stalls, and \(f_{\text{stall}}\), the fraction of
baseline execution cycles exposed to L1D miss latency. Categorical presets are
reported as deltas or speedup against a per-workload baseline
(\(\mathrm{\textsc{ipc}}/\mathrm{\textsc{ipc}}_{\text{baseline}}\)); numeric
sweeps are plotted as absolute values, with logarithmic scales where needed.

\subsection{Workload Selection}\label{sub:workloads}

The five PolyBench Version~4.2.1 \cite{polybench:2016} kernels were chosen to
combine differently the two mechanisms we identify as decisive: access-pattern
spatiality and working-set size relative to the hierarchy; so the suite
contains kernels satisfying neither, one, or both conditions;
Table~\ref{tab:workloads} gives their datasets and working sets. \textsc{gemm} computes the dense product \(C \leftarrow \beta C +
\alpha AB\), a core \textsc{blas} operation \cite{lawson:1979}, whose inner loop
reuses the \(C_{ij}\) accumulator across the entire \(k\)-reduction.
\textsc{ata}x computes \(y = A^{T}(A x)\), combining row-major streaming with
column-wise accesses in the transpose phase, yielding mixed spatial locality and
limited temporal reuse. Floyd-Warshall computes
all-pairs shortest paths by dynamic programming over a 2D matrix
\cite{floyd:1962, warshall:1962}, with dense, regular accesses and strong
temporal reuse across the outer loop. Jacobi-2D applies a five-point stencil over a 2D
grid \cite{golub:2013}, giving structured spatial locality at offsets \(\pm 1\) and
\(\pm N\). Seidel-2D applies a nine-point in-place Gauss--Seidel update whose
immediate reuse of new values introduces loop-carried dependencies, increasing
temporal locality but reducing parallelism \cite{golub:2013}. All are \(O(n^3)\) in time
and \(O(n^2)\) in memory except \textsc{ata}x, which is \(O(n^2)\) in both.
Dataset sizes were reduced iteratively until each workload completed within a
practical budget (on the order of days); Floyd-Warshall, with cubic complexity and
a large constant factor, required \texttt{SMALL\_\-DATA\-SET}.

Finally, the end-to-end workflow is one ordered, idempotent build graph in the Zig

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Overview and Ranking of the Three Factors}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} summarizes the entire sweep as how much \textsc{ipc}
each factor buys per kernel. Line size moves \textsc{ipc} by up to \(15.4\%\),
capacity by up to \(7.2\%\), and associativity above direct-mapped by at most
\(0.2\%\). Two features guide this section: the kernel ordering is the same for
line size and capacity, suggesting one underlying quantity governs both; and the
mechanism is uniform where the outcome is not, since L1D \textsc{mpki} falls almost
exactly \(8.0\times\) for four of five kernels while \textsc{ipc} gains span
\(0.5\%\) to \(15.4\%\).

The cache behaves identically; what differs is each kernel's exposure to it,
measured by \(f_{\text{stall}}\), the fraction of baseline cycles spent stalled on
L1D misses (last column of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). It ranges from \(0.004\)
for Floyd-Warshall to \(0.090\) for \textsc{ata}x, a 25-fold spread, and orders the
kernels identically for both sweeps: an identical \(8.0\times\) miss reduction
buys little where there is little stall time to recover. Treating the line-size
sweep as Amdahl's law over the stalled fraction,
\(\mathrm{speedup} = 1/(1 - k\,f_{\text{stall}})\) with \(k\) the fraction of
misses eliminated (\(\approx0.875\)), predicts every measured speedup to within
1.8~percentage points. Each result below is the same mechanism at a different
\(f_{\text{stall}}\).

\subsection{Cache Line Size Effects}\label{sub:cacheline}

Line size acts through the same geometric mechanism in all five kernels: a wider
line amortizes one tag lookup and one refill over more contiguous words, so for
sequential access the miss count falls in inverse proportion to line width. Hence
the near-exact \(8.0\times\) reduction in L1D \textsc{mpki} from 32 to 256 bytes
(Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_line}) in every kernel but \textsc{ata}x, where it is
\(7.6\times\). What differs is how much that uniform response is worth, and two
independent conditions govern its value.

The first is that the access pattern be genuinely spatial rather than already
served by temporal reuse. \textsc{gemm} fails it despite a perfect \(8.0\times\)
miss reduction: its inner loop reuses the \(C_{ij}\) accumulator across the entire
\(k\)-reduction, so the accesses dominating its execution are already L1D hits
regardless of line width. Only \(3.4\%\) of its baseline cycles are
stall-exposed, capping the gain at \(+5.8\%\) (\textsc{cpi} \(7.14\) to \(6.75\)). \textsc{ata}x is the opposite: its forward
phase streams a contiguous \(1900\times2100\) matrix with almost no reuse, so
nearly every access can be spatially amortized, converting the miss reduction into
\(+15.4\%\) \textsc{ipc}. Jacobi-2D falls
between, touching neighbors at offsets \(\pm 1\) and \(\pm N\) of which only the
former is contiguous, and gains \(+6.6\%\).

The second is that the working set be large enough, relative to the hierarchy, for
the kernel to be miss-bound at all. Floyd-Warshall fails it decisively: it achieves
the full \(8.0\times\) miss reduction and reuses the \(path\) matrix heavily, yet
\textsc{ipc} moves only \(+0.5\%\), because at \texttt{SMALL\_\-DATA\-SET} its
entire 127~KiB footprint (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) already resides in the 256~KiB
baseline L2 and just \(0.4\%\) of its cycles are stall-exposed---an artifact of
our dataset reduction, not a property of the algorithm.

Seidel-2D shows why absolute latency reductions must not be read as performance.
It has one of the largest absolute drops in total miss latency in the suite,
\(162\times10^9\) to \(20\times10^9\) ticks (\(-87.5\%\)), yet gains only \(+2.2\%\) \textsc{ipc}: its
\(f_{\text{stall}}\) is just \(0.014\), since it executes \(2.0\times10^9\)
instructions over a 1.22~MiB grid and is overwhelmingly compute-bound, and its
in-place update carries loop-carried dependencies, so removing memory stalls
exposes a dependency bound rather than releasing issue slots. A large relative change in a memory metric is thus
compatible with negligible performance change whenever that metric governed a
small share of execution time. No kernel failing either condition was rescued by a
larger line, nor did any satisfying both fail to benefit.

\subsection{Associativity: A Structurally Low-Leverage Parameter}\label{sub:assoc}

Associativity's effect is not merely small but structurally bounded for this
workload class, for a reason that generalizes beyond our five kernels. It addresses
conflict misses specifically, letting several lines that index to the same set
coexist. Our kernels access memory at unit stride, or at a fixed offset of \(N\)
for the stencils, so successive addresses spread roughly uniformly over the index
bits and few simultaneously-live addresses alias to any single set. Their misses
are dominated by compulsory and capacity effects: a parameter targeting a miss
category the workload barely generates cannot help, however generously
provisioned.

The data bear this out with almost no residual. At L1, \textsc{ipc} varies by at
most \(0.18\%\) (\textsc{ata}x) and by \(0.00\%\) for Jacobi-2D and Seidel-2D
across the entire 2-to-16-way range. Deeper levels
are flatter still: the L2 sweep moves \textsc{ipc} by \(0.00\%\) in all five
kernels and the L3 sweep by at most \(0.0007\%\), as expected once L1 has absorbed
the conflict pressure. The sole exception is the direct-mapped case, where with one
way every alias necessarily evicts: a direct-mapped L1D carries a higher miss rate
than an 8-way one---\(+96\%\) in Floyd-Warshall, \(+31\%\) in \textsc{gemm},
\(+19\%\) in Jacobi-2D, \(+11\%\) in Seidel-2D, \(+3\%\) in \textsc{ata}x---yet
even these are largely invisible in \textsc{ipc}, since eliminating
Floyd-Warshall's \(96\%\) penalty is worth \(+0.29\%\). One additional way
captures essentially all the conflict pressure these kernels produce; further
associativity is wasted area.

\subsection{Capacity: A Threshold Effect, Not a Continuum}\label{sub:capacity}

Capacity is neither uniform (like line size) nor inert (like associativity) but a
\textit{threshold} effect, active only when the working set crosses the
\textsc{llc} boundary, as the agreement between the footprints in
Table~\ref{tab:workloads} and the capacity column of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}
shows. \textsc{ata}x is the only kernel whose footprint (30.5~MiB, \(3.8\times\)
the baseline \textsc{llc}) exceeds the hierarchy, and the only one with a
substantial response: \(+5.9\%\) for the Ryzen~5600X and 7700X presets (32~MiB L3)
and \(+7.2\%\) for Large Server (64~MiB L3), precisely the configurations whose
\textsc{llc} holds a meaningful fraction of matrix \(A\). The other four have
footprints of \(0.12\)--\(0.15\times\) the baseline \textsc{llc} and gain at most
\(+2.7\%\), because they already fit.

Two aspects are easy to misread. First, the effect is non-monotonic in nominal
capacity: for \textsc{ata}x the i9-9900K preset (16~MiB L3) yields no gain while
the Ryzen~5600X (32~MiB) yields \(+5.9\%\), because what matters is whether the
\textsc{llc} crosses the kernel's reuse-distance threshold, not total bytes, so
ranking presets by aggregate size would mispredict performance. Second, the one
negative result runs in the expected direction: Seidel-2D loses \(3.1\%\)
\textsc{ipc} under Small Embedded, the only preset shrinking the hierarchy below
baseline (16~KiB L1, 128~KiB L2, 1~MiB L3); its 1.22~MiB grid exceeds that 1~MiB L3
and the L3 miss rate jumps from \(7\times10^{-6}\) to \(1.0\)---every L3 access
misses. This is the threshold mechanism in reverse, confirming that the boundary,
not the magnitude, governs the effect. Because datasets were reduced for
tractability, four of five kernels sit on the insensitive side of that threshold, so
our capacity effects are lower bounds.

Two further null results deserve record. First, instruction-cache traffic is free: across
all 155 simulations the L1I never exceeded 78 misses (at most \(0.34\)
\textsc{mpki}), because by the time the binary calls \textit{libm5} to begin
collecting statistics, the kernel's few kilobytes of instructions have been fetched
and fit trivially in any L1I we simulate. L1I is therefore insensitive to every
swept parameter and we exclude it. This is a general pitfall of
\textsc{roi}-scoped measurement rather than a property of these kernels: any study
instrumenting a tight numerical loop will find its instruction-side statistics
pre-warmed, and should not conclude that instruction fetch is unimportant in the
full application. Second, dataset size is a confound: part of the cross-kernel
variation in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reflects dataset choice rather than
intrinsic behavior, most consequentially at the extremes, since \textsc{ata}x is
the only kernel at \texttt{LARGE\_\-DATA\-SET} and shows the largest response
while Floyd-Warshall is the only one at \texttt{SMALL\_\-DATA\-SET} and shows the
smallest. The mechanism is nevertheless identified, since dataset size acts on
\(f_{\text{stall}}\) by changing the working set relative to the hierarchy:
sensitivity is a property of the pair (kernel, dataset), which is why
Table~\ref{tab:workloads} reports footprints.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The chief product of this work is a transferable criterion for deciding
\textit{a priori} which cache parameter is worth tuning for a given regular
numerical kernel. Essentially all observed line-size and capacity sensitivity is
explained by whether a kernel jointly satisfies two conditions: (i)~its access
pattern is genuinely spatial rather than already absorbed by temporal reuse or
compute intensity, and (ii)~its working set is large enough, relative to the
hierarchy, that the kernel is miss-bound at all. \textsc{ata}x satisfies both and
responds strongly to line size (\(+15.4\%\)) and capacity (\(+7.2\%\));
\textsc{gemm} fails~(i) through its \(C_{ij}\) reuse and Floyd-Warshall fails~(ii)
with a 127~KiB footprint inside a 256~KiB L2. The criterion is operational, not
merely explanatory: both conditions reduce to \(f_{\text{stall}}\), so a designer
facing a new regular kernel can estimate its sensitivity from one baseline
measurement rather than a sweep. Associativity needs no such estimate, being
structurally low-leverage for this workload class because it targets conflict
misses and regular strides generate few conflicts.

Several limitations bound these claims. The in-order core captures neither
out-of-order execution nor memory-level parallelism, so our sensitivities are upper
bounds and the Amdahl-style model remains untested where stalls are overlapped
rather than exposed. Dataset reduction leaves four of five kernels below the
\textsc{llc} threshold, making the capacity effects lower bounds, and the workloads
are regular by construction, so the criterion's applicability to irregular
applications remains open. Because the pipeline is a deterministic, idempotent build
graph with pinned versions, it can be re-run, audited, or extended at low marginal
cost \cite{repo:2026}: future work may scale datasets so all kernels straddle the
\textsc{llc} threshold, add irregular applications, sweep replacement and
prefetching policies, and re-run the grid on an out-of-order core.
